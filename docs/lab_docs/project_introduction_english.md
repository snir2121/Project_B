## Introduction (p. 3)

## Laboratory safety instructions (pp. 4–7)

### General

These safety instructions are provided to students to prevent accidents while carrying out projects and working in the Faculty of Electrical Engineering's High-Speed Digital Systems Laboratory. They draw attention to risks associated with laboratory activities, in order to prevent injuries and damage. Read them carefully and follow them.

### Laboratory safety framework

- A project may not be undertaken in a specialized laboratory without written approval from the Safety Unit confirming a passing grade in the safety course.
- At the beginning of the semester, laboratory staff will brief all students on safety instructions, laboratory procedures, and the laboratory guide.
- Laboratory staff are responsible for safety arrangements in your working area. Listen to and obey their instructions.
- **Do not work alone in the laboratory.**
- Some activities may be performed only with the supervisor/laboratory staff or after appropriate training. See *Safety and security rules at the development station*.

### Do's and don'ts

- Inform staff about any hazardous situation or defect in the laboratory or its immediate surroundings.
- Do not perform any action that may endanger people present.
- Every device and instrument has an intended purpose; improvisations are a major cause of accidents.
- Practical jokes may cause accidents.
- No smoking, food, or drinks in the laboratory.
- Leave the workbench clean and tidy after work.

### Electrical safety

- The laboratory is equipped with residual-current protection devices; these do **not** provide complete protection against electric shock.
- Laboratory staff have received electrical safety training and hold the required electrician certification. Contact them and other professionals in emergencies.
- **You are authorized to work only at extra-low voltage, up to 15 V DC.**
- Do not use measurement instruments, power supplies, or other equipment with an expired service/inspection label.
- When connecting or disconnecting electrical equipment, hold the plug rather than pulling the cable, and keep your hands dry.
- Verify the absence of voltage with a functioning measuring instrument that was checked before the power was disconnected.
- Do not unplug a power cable from an appliance while leaving its other end connected to mains power: **electric shock hazard**.
- Do not use defective electrical equipment or accessories:
  - Do not operate equipment from a defective wall socket.
  - Do not use equipment connected via flexible power cables with damaged insulation.
  - Do not use damaged or improperly secured plugs.
  - Do not repair or disassemble electrical equipment, including fuses installed inside devices.
  - Report defects to staff, clearly mark the defective device, and describe the fault.

### Emergency power-off button

- The laboratory has an emergency power-disconnect button in **Room 353** (the last room), near the entrance and next to the light switches.
- In an emergency, activate this button immediately.
- If the button fails to disconnect power, use the main switch in the electrical cabinet. Do not open that cabinet under other circumstances. Main switches are marked in red.
- There is one electrical cabinet, near the entrance door inside Room 353; it serves the entire laboratory. The Room 353 emergency button disconnects the power supplied to this cabinet.
- **Important:** sockets labeled in blue *“Supplied by UPS”* are **not** controlled by the emergency button. They are disconnected by switches in the UPS electrical panel in the printer room.

### Fire safety

- Laboratory staff have completed fire-safety training. Obtain help from them and other professionals during an emergency.
- Consult the safety noticeboard for the emergency reporting procedure and instructions for extinguishing fires and responding to a fire.
- Safety noticeboards are located in the laboratory entrance corridor and laboratory rooms.
- The laboratory has a central fire-extinguishing station and automatic sprinklers. The fire-extinguishing station is in the central room beside the emergency exit; sprinklers activate automatically.
- Use fire extinguishers **only in an emergency**.

### Resuscitation and first aid

- Laboratory staff have first-aid and resuscitation training; seek their help and professional assistance in emergencies.
- Consult safety noticeboards for emergency reporting and assistance to electric-shock victims. This information may save lives.
- Two first-aid kits are in the laboratory: **Room 317-0 (with Eli)** and **Room 318 (with Ina)**.
- Use first-aid supplies only in emergencies.

### Emergency exits

- The large open-space laboratory has an emergency exit door and fire-extinguishing station in its center.
- The emergency exit key is in an emergency box adjacent to the door. Using it activates an emergency alarm.
- For an emergency requiring evacuation, such as fire, leave immediately through the nearest accessible emergency exit.

## Project management (p. 15)

How a project is managed is extremely important: poor resource management can delay submission or cause failure. The supervisor's role is to offer professional engineering advice; the students' most important responsibility is to manage the project properly.

1. Manage the project **from day one**. Planning errors are easy to correct at the beginning and very difficult to change at the end. The beginning is, in practice, the most important part.
2. Clearly define **what the project is intended to improve or fix**. Only when you understand what needs changing should you define the method.
3. Prefer the simplest method that achieves the goal. Once the goal is defined, identify the simplest solution.
4. Develop a system architecture implementing the solution: break the overall system into functional subsystems, define their interactions clearly, and document them in a **block diagram**.
5. List tasks required for each part. Identify dependencies and ordering, estimate durations, and create a **Gantt chart**. Consult the supervisor at this stage.
6. Divide tasks between project partners, taking account of each person's strengths and weaknesses.
7. Prepare a detailed **weekly schedule for each partner**, allowing for potential delays such as reserve military duty or exams.
8. Start according to the schedule, and update any task that falls behind so that progress is continuously monitored.

A weekly supervisor meeting is very important: it helps the supervisor determine whether the project is on track and identify design mistakes early, before they cause damage or delays.

## Guidelines for project presentations (p. 16)

Prepare presentations using **PowerPoint**.

### Slide design

1. Minimum font size: **24 pt**. Text should contrast with the background.
2. No more than **5–7 words per line**.
3. No more than **10 lines per slide**.
4. Minimum line spacing: **twice the font size**.
5. Leave approximately **1 cm margins** on every side.
6. Color is encouraged, but use **no more than four colors per slide**.
7. Diagrams, illustrations, and drawings are recommended; graphics and their labels must be clear.
8. Animations may be used but are not required.

### Recommended presentation structure

1. Title slide: project name, student names, supervisor, semester, and presentation type.
2. Project objectives.
3. Theoretical background.
4. Project block diagram.
5. Explanation of each block.
6. Software architecture.
7. Schedule.
8. Summary and conclusions.

Adapt the structure to the presentation type. For example, the midterm presentation emphasizes the schedule and gives less weight to conclusions. The final presentation omits the project schedule and emphasizes summary and conclusions. Consult the supervisor and laboratory staff before preparing the slides.

## Specification presentation (p. 17)

This is the first required presentation. Its central elements are the project's objectives and execution schedule. Project success is evaluated largely by whether the final deliverables meet the objectives defined here.

Present these subjects **in this order**:

1. Project name and number; presentation type; semester; supervisor name; student names.
2. Project background and motivation.
3. Project goals stated in **measurable terms**.
4. Possible development environments and components/platforms for development.
5. Overall **hardware and software block diagram**.
6. Explanation of every block.
7. Definition of the milestone to be reached by mid-semester.
8. Detailed **weekly Gantt chart** for all tasks up to the midterm presentation.

Usually, the expected midterm milestone is **proof of feasibility**.

## Mid-semester presentation (p. 17)

The midterm presentation finalizes the project objectives, architecture, development environments, and Gantt chart through completion. It should be precise and detailed. Its purpose is to obtain feedback on the design and improve it if necessary.

Include:

1. Project name and number; presentation type; semester; supervisor; student names.
2. Background and motivation.
3. **Measurable project goals**.
4. Development environments and components used.
5. Overall hardware/software block diagram.
6. Detailed hardware/software block diagram.
7. Explanation of every block.
8. Detailed **weekly Gantt chart** for all tasks through project completion.

## Project report (pp. 18–19)

The report documents the project in detail for future students who may continue developing it. Include the following sections:

1. **Cover information:** Project name and number; project type; semester; supervisor; student names.
2. **Abstract:** A short account (a few lines) of the work, general operating principle, broad requirements, and methods. Include the main facts: what was done, how it was done, key results, and their significance. The abstract should be clear and understandable.
3. **Introduction:** Purpose and role of the project; relationship to other work; required special features, practical performance, and possible solutions.
4. **Theoretical background:** Relevant theory for the chosen solution, including equations and a thorough account of strengths and weaknesses.
5. **Hardware description**, at two levels:
   - Block-diagram level, dividing the project into components and explaining each block's function, inputs, and outputs.
   - Electrical schematic level (the schematic itself belongs in the appendices).
   - Add voltage waveform diagrams for analog parts and timing diagrams for digital parts. Include all state-machine diagrams. Component datasheets need **not** be included here.
6. **Software description**, at two levels:
   - Process level.
   - Code level (put actual code in an appendix if required).
7. **Summary and conclusions:** Findings, including tables, charts, etc. Compare requirements to actual results. Explain possible improvements and additional features that could improve performance or capabilities.

### Appendices

- Complete and updated hardware schematics.
- Software code.
- FPGA programming code.
- **Operation instructions:** Explain how to run the project for a person who has never seen it before.

**Notes:** Include relevant code excerpts alongside explanations in the report; provide the **complete code electronically only**. You do not need to print the report. Submit it electronically to the supervisor and laboratory staff. The report must be written in **English** except in special cases; industry-collaboration projects must be in English. The final report filename must also be in English.

1. Present the final project to laboratory staff.

   |---|---|

   - A small **featured image** representing the project, different from the images in the poster.
   - English text/HTML written **only in the third person**:
     - Short project abstract of **1–2 sentences**.
     - Longer project abstract of approximately **half to three-quarters of a page**.
   - Obtain the supervisor's approval of website text/files.
6. In **Labadmin**, submit the project and upload the report, naming it **`Report.pdf`** with display name **`Final Report`**.
7. Return all borrowed laboratory equipment to **Ina Rivkin**. **This step is not required at the end of Part A of a two-semester project.**
